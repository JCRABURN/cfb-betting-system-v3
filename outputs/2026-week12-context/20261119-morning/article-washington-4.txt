No. 93, Forever: Hunter Green Carries His Father’s Legacy Onto The Field
November 18, 2026
|
Football
Share:
By:
MARK MOSCHETTI
SEATTLE
– Before he takes the field for a University of Washington football game,
Hunter Green
always takes a knee.
It's not necessarily in prayer. But it's always in conversation …
… with Dad.
"To me, I'm just talking with him, closing my eyes and praying – or whatever you want to call it," the 23-year-old Husky punter said. "I try to envision him, envision him standing in front of me, and just tell him I miss him and tell him I'm so grateful to have people (from among family and friends) attending games. My mom is coming to every game this year – it helps to have family at games.
"But I also ask him to give me all the power that he has and help me to perform on Saturday," Green added.
The man with whom Hunter is conversing is Phil Green, a walk-on kicker who later moved to safety who was part of the 1991 UW national championship team. In July 2025, Green died at the age of 55 after a seven-year battle with ALS.
More commonly known as Lou Gehrig's Disease in recognition of the New York Yankees great who lost his life to it in 1941 at the age of 37, ALS is a progressive neurogenerative disease that gradually causes patients to lose the ability to control their muscles – but usually does not rob them of their mental capacity.
On Saturday when the Huskies tangle at home with defending national champion Indiana, that 1991 Washington squad will be honored as it approaches the 35
th
anniversary of its own national title.
Hunter, who transferred to Washington this year from San Diego State and already has broken the UW single-season record with seven punts of 60-plus yards, will be there for the ceremony. As always, he'll be wearing No. 93 – same as his dad.
He already knows that Phil, in his own spiritual way, will be right alongside him.
"I know he's watching and I know he would be proud. It would be so different if he were here," Hunter said. "But it's just knowing that somehow, some way, he is watching."
LOTS OF SPORTS – BUT NOT TACKLE FOOTBALL
Growing up just across the water from UW in Redmond,
Hunter Green
watched all kinds of sports with his father while growing up, which included attending Huskies games. But they also would watch soccer, the NFL, baseball – even the Tour de France, the world's premier bicycle race, which he still tunes into each year.
"That's something that rubbed off on me – every July, I'm watching the Tour de France when that rolls around," he said. "That's something I looked at with him, and it's something I still like to do.
"If there was a sport and it was either on TV or we could buy the equipment, we either had the equipment or we were watching."
Ironically, while he played different sports as a kid, tackle football was not one of them. Phil limited him to the flag version of the game. It wasn't until Hunter began his freshman year at Lake Washington High School that he was finally able to put on the pads.
"He didn't let me play youth football just because of the risk of head injuries," said Green, who also regularly played soccer. (Phil was an influence there, as well, having played semipro soccer for a couple of years and trying out with the New England Revolution of Major League Soccer.) "He suffered a lot of concussions when he played and kind of knew the effects of it so didn't want it to happen to me.
"When high school rolled around, he was like, 'All right, you can play kicker or punter because you're not going to get hit.'"
Turns out that was a perfect fit for Hunter.
"Maybe if I had more determination and loved running back and needed to do that, he probably would have let me try," he said. "But that's something that never appealed to me."
Green hadn't initially thought about playing college football. But by his junior year at Lake Washington, he was warming up to the idea that perhaps he could. He wound up with a partial scholarship to Northern Colorado and spent three years there (redshirting as a freshman in 2022).
The next fall, Green started punting as well as kicking, averaging 43.5 yards on 68 attempts. In 2024, he raised that to 45.5 yards, added nine field goals, was an All-Big Sky pick and a third-team FCS All-American.
HEADING BACK HOME
With Phil and his wife living about an hour outside of San Diego, Green transferred to San Diego State for the 2025 season. Phil never got to see him play there, having passed a few weeks prior to the first game. But before that, Hunter would drive up to visit often – and would see his dad working diligently with others in the ALS community even as his own fight against the disease was in its final stages.
"I would go downstairs in the morning, and he was always on a phone call with different people, trying to figure thigs out," Green said. "His work ethic and his attitude, waking up every day and pushing himself both mentally and physically – that's something I admire and something I miss."
After averaging 47 yards on 63 punts for the Aztecs, Green still had one year of eligibility remaining (from his redshirt freshman year at Northern Colorado) and knew where he wanted to spend it.
"I knew there was going to be interest. San Diego is Group of 6, and I knew there was interest from Power 4 teams," Green said. "I had one year left, knowing I wanted to be a Washington Husky where my dad played."
That interest was mutual on the Montlake side – more than mutual, as special teams coordinator
Chris Petrilli
described it.
"I've known about him for about two years prior (to Green transferring here)," he said. "You keep track of kids and go, 'Maybe if this guy is ever in the portal, this would be one we'd look at.'
Green was in the portal. Petrilli and the UW coaching staff saw his name.
"You find out he's local, you find out who is dad was, you find out the family dynamic. This was a no-brainer," Petrilli said. "I called his private coach and said, 'Hey, is this a good kid?' Everybody and his dog vouched for him, and he has been all that – and 10 times that – since getting here."
It's not every day that a 235-pound, 6-foot-4 punter drops in. Green knows his size is a plus, as he has those seven kicks that have gone 60-plus yards, 13 more of 50-plus, four games with averages of 50-plus, and 18 of his 50 attempts being downed inside the 20, plus three touchbacks.
But there's more to his task than just booming it.
"Obviously, I have the physical size, and it helps having a little more weight behind the ball," he said. "But mentally, you have to be ready for stuff, or the elements, testing yourself knowing you can do it in the wind, rain, or snow. Also mentally, it's just playing free, not overthinking things, just trusting yourself, knowing you can do it and trying not to overcomplicate or trying to do too much."
Petrilli enjoys working with Green on both the technical and mental aspects.
"He's savage about his craft, hyper-attentive to details, and I can coach him really hard," Petrilli said. "But he's an elite punter on his own – I'm not trying to re-invent him."
THE NUMBER FITS … AND HE WEARS IT
Playing his final season closer to home and at his dad's alma mater checked two big boxes for Green.
Another one still needed to be checked:
Number 93.
It hadn't been available to him at Northern Colorado, where he wore 37. At San Diego State, he was able to switch to it prior to the season.
But at Washington, "it was open. (The player who wore it in 2025 was no longer in the program.) When I was transferring in, that was one of the deal-breakers. I said, 'I need this number.'"
"I didn't have to negotiate, luckily," Green said. "If I had, I would have asked why that number was important to them if they had 93. I would do anything to try to get it."
Having that number will never be more meaningful to him than it will on Saturday. In addition to recognizing the 1991 team, it also will be Senior Day prior to the 12:30 p.m. kickoff against No. 6-ranked Indiana.
"It's super special," Green said. "When they would do anniversary stuff like they're doing for this game, I would go with my dad and family to those games. I'd meet his former teammates, and it was always so cool how he interacted with those guys.
"It'll be an opportunity to talk to them, or even just meet them and get to know them and see the guys my dad battled next to in the weight room and on the practice file. I'm really excited."
Of course, since Hunter is just 23, he wasn't even around to see those battles in person. But even if he had been, it couldn't compare to the way he watched his dad battle ALS.
"It's amazing to me to look back at it and seeing what he did," Green said. "Almost immediately after the diagnosis (it was 2018; Hunter was 14 at the time), he tried to get in touch with people. He tried to find community and outlets and avenues where he could not only find something that could possibly help him. In most cases, he was trying to do the most he could to help others going through the same disease."
Petrilli shared a particularly poignant moment from Washington's 17-10 win at Nebraska on Oct. 31.
Green punted eight times. Of those, five pinned the Cornhuskers inside their 20, twice at their own 5. The farthest up the field they started was at their 34 on Green's first punt of the night. The return might have gone even farther had Green himself not stepped up and put a hard-but-clean tackle on the Nebraska returner coming up the sideline.
Immediately afterward, "I'm running up to him and going crazy because that's what I do," Petrilli said. "He said, 'Man, you look just like a proud dad – you're so fired up.' It kind of hits you heavy, like, 'Wow, what a compliment.'
"I do view these guys as an extension of my family," Petrilli added. "My dad died when I was young and I didn't get to play football in front of him, and I went to his alma mater (Carroll College in Helena, Montana). There are these similarities in our stories. It's heavy, but it's so awesome. He lights up when he talks about his dad and his family."
Most certainly,
Hunter Green
also lights up when he talks
to
his dad …
… every time he takes a knee before he takes the field.
