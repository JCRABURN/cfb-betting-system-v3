Spotlight Bull: Grayson “Pup” Howard Knows the Meaning of Sacrifice
November 11, 2026
|
Football
Share:
Joey Johnston
/
Athletics Senior Writer
For University of South Florida linebacker
Grayson Howard
— everyone calls him "Pup'' — college football hasn't worked out the way he imagined. He has battled through a litany of injuries. He's on his third school.
Really, it has always been about persistence. Howard learned that lesson long ago. There are moments of glory, of course, such as his 13-tackle performance last week, more evidence that things are trending well. It was the fourth consecutive time he has led the Bulls in that category. But mostly, it's about what you do when no one is watching, how you react when things don't go your way.
Howard was trained well by his father, his role model and the best man he has ever known. For 22 years, Gregory Howard was in the U.S. Navy, rising to the rank of Chief Petty Officer, making constant sacrifices and sometimes finding himself halfway around the world while family milestones or holiday were celebrated back home.
When the Bulls (6-3, 2-3) meet the Memphis Tigers (7-2, 4-1) in Thursday night's ESPN American Conference showdown at Raymond James Stadium, it's South Florida's annual "Salute To Service'' game. The Bulls will be outfitted in special garb with red, white and blue accents. Each branch of the military will be recognized with patriotic music. Like always, the crowd will cheer loudly and show the proper respect.
For Howard, the night isn't just ceremonial. It means more.
"There's no bigger sacrifice than the ones made by people in the military,'' said Howard (6-foot-4, 240 pounds), the youngest of six siblings. "They literally put their lives on the line for all of us. When you're in the military, when you're deployed on a mission, you don't know if you'll return home. For fans at this game, they might be cheering for people they don't know, people they'll never meet. But for me, it's real.
"My dad is one of the best role models I've ever had in my life. I've seen the way he wakes up, straps his boots on and goes to work. No matter how many hours he has to work, he provides for his family. He's just a great man. I'm so blessed to be able to have a father like that.''
Howard's father, who now works as a police officer at Jacksonville's Naval Station Mayport, taught lessons that still resonate.
Be on time.
Work hard.
Don't complain.
And there's only one way to start each day — by making your bed.
"At first, my kids didn't understand this, how picking up after yourself in a disciplined way affects you more than you think,'' said Howard's father, who retired from active service in 2012. "It teaches you how to lead. It organizes your mind. And it lets you know that you began the day by accomplishing something. Maybe making your bed is the only thing you're going to accomplish. But it's something. And that goes a long way.''
"Even though my dad isn't watching, I'm still making my bed,'' Howard said with a smile. "I might get a little slack here and there. But I try to make my bed good and tight. If my dad comes to visit, walks in and that bed isn't made to the right standard, I'll hear about it. It might seem like a little thing, but it's about holding yourself to a standard and doing things the right way.''
In life.
And in football.
"I think Pup's greatest quality is his mind,'' Bulls defensive coordinator Josh Aldridge said. "I mean, he's a great athlete, but man, that guy does so much for our defense in terms of getting people lined up. He's an extension of me out there. He operates our defense at an elite level. He's one of the best I've been around.
"If there's an inexperienced guy out there, I just tell them to look over at Pup because he's always doing the right thing. Just the energy and preparation he brings on a daily basis, it's impressive. It's a great example. You just notice the talent and the attitude right away.''
Conditioned For Excellence
The coach at Jacksonville's Andrew Jackson High School noticed Howard right away — and that prompted the nickname.
Howard was just an eighth-grader, practicing in spring ball with the Jackson High varsity.
"The coach said he was going to call me 'Pup' because he said he knew one day that I was going to be a big dog,'' Howard said. "It stuck. Since I was in the ninth grade, 'Pup' was what people called me.''
Howard's father said he always felt his youngest son had a special destiny. Howard was a "disciplined baby'' with an easy-going temperament — a pleasure, really. In school, he got constant accolades from teachers and graduated from Jackson High with a 4.7 grade-point average, rarely making a B. He also dabbled in music.
"I was the Pop Warner football coach and Grayson was also playing the piano at the time,'' Howard's father said. "At one point, he didn't want continue playing football. He wanted to concentrate on the piano.
"I told him, 'Look, I'm coaching. You're brother has to be here. You're not just staying home. You're going to do both.' So he stuck with football, maybe a little reluctantly. But pretty soon after that, it became his passion.''
As a sixth-grader, he was cut from the middle-school team and returned to Pop Warner. But on his own, Howard approached the middle-school coach and asked, "What do I have to do to make your team next year?'' Howard took the advice to heart. In the seventh and eighth grades, Howard was the team's star. His confidence was boosted further when NFL players came to Mayport for a football camp. They were very complimentary about Howard's potential.
At Jackson High, Howard became a four-star prospect and the nation's No. 18-ranked linebacker while leading his team to its best season in a dozen years. Howard played three seasons in the SEC, originally signing with South Carolina, then coming closer to home with the Florida Gators. After starting nine games in 2024 with UF, he suffered a high-ankle sprain last season that required surgery and never allowed him to fully recover.
South Florida looked like the perfect spot because he loved the program's upside, plus the approach of head coach Brian Hartline and his staff. During winter conditioning drills, Howard was selected as the Iron Bull, "given to the student-athlete who best exemplifies unquestionable physical training dedication, determination, discipline, toughness and leadership.''
In USF's Sept. 5 opener against FIU, on the game's fourth play, Howard was hurt again. After spraining the MCL in his left knee, Howard missed the next two games completely and was very limited in a third. For most players, that would prompt maddening frustration. For Howard, it was just another chapter that tested his resolve.
"I never turn my back on my beliefs and my faith in God,'' Howard said. "I definitely believe it's all for a reason. It has made me into a stronger human being. I feel like I'm far ahead of where I was two years ago as a man.
"A lot of people might complain or think the world is against them. They want to run from adversity. I'm actually thankful for it. All the ups and downs, they make you into a better person. Getting hurt again (at USF), it did nothing but make me stronger.''
Pup Can Train Dogs, Too
If Howard ever feels sorry for himself, he'll snap back into form after a few minutes of absorbing the unconditional love from Knox, his 77-pound black Labrador Retriever, a rescue dog that he got from Tampa's Humane Society.
Howard had a childhood dog and fostered two others while he lived in Gainesville. This time, he knew he wanted to adopt a dog. Howard learned training techniques from YouTube videos and ChatGPT, gradually transforming Knox from hyper to more disciplined.
"The more structure he has, the more freedom he gets,'' Howard said. "I love him to death and it has taught me a lot, like patience and love. It's a nice little side hobby to keep my mind off football at times.
"It has enforced that lesson I've known all along, but it's a great reminder. We all chase instant gratification, right? Training a dog reminds you that all good things take a little time. He's the one learning, but he has taught me a lot. We've built him into a solid place with lots of trust. He's like my best friend. He was in the shelter and I was able to give him a second chance. If I'm away for him at any time, then I show up, it's like his whole world stops. When he's looking at me, it's like he's saying, 'That's my daddy right there. That's my daddy.' ''
The leadership Howard shows in training his dog, he feels a similar responsibility with the Bulls. He receives his direction from Aldridge and implements the strategy to younger players. When Aldridge says, "Give us an inch and we'll defend it,'' Howard takes that advice to heart and makes sure his teammates understand that mentality.
"Pup is a leader and he's very relentless,'' Bulls safety
Tavin Ward
said. "Even after he got hurt a few plays into the season, he didn't allow that injury to stop him from being a leader. He was always around, building guys up, especially in the linebacker room. When he's out there on the field, the way he approaches it is just contagious.''
"When you're a defensive player particularly, after you spend some time together, you feel a love for your brothers,'' Howard said. "The bond grows. You play harder for each other. There's a camaraderie that you feel, probably something that gets replicated in very few places.''
One of those places is the military.
Howard's father, originally from Americus, Ga., can relate to that sentiment.
"The thing I enjoyed the most about the military was the camaraderie,'' Howard's father said. "We might've had differences of opinion, but when it came to teamwork and being one, we all worked together.
"We all chose that kind of life for the love of our country and to preserve our freedom. We knew what we were getting into. Sometimes, it's very tough. Your child is sick. Your family member is going through something. And you're not there. But you have to focus on the bigger objective.''
It's similar to football. Salute To Service isn't just a patriotic gesture for Howard. It's a reminder of his father, all those years of service and how the family was raised. When Howard steps on the field, it's more than a game. It's a reflection of the values he learned and the responsibility he always feels. Just like his father, Howard wants to carry himself with maturity and honor.
Red, white and true.
Joey Johnston
Athletics Senior Writer
