Two knee surgeries and a goal written on his mirror later, Ibrahim Diawara will walk out on Senior Day as a Coastal Carolina captain
By:
Mike Uva
Ibrahim Diawara
had only been at Coastal Carolina for two and a half weeks when his right knee gave out.
The previous fall, he felt it pop while making a tackle against Kutztown on a cold, wet day. He played through the pain for the rest of the season at Division II Shippensburg University in Pennsylvania, then transferred to Coastal.
He barely knew his new teammates when he found out he had a torn meniscus. He talked it over with his family and decided to have it repaired. That meant missing all of spring practice.
Diawara had been through a knee injury before. In eighth grade, he injured the growth plate in his left knee while playing youth football for the Southwest Wolves in Philadelphia. After that, he walked away from football.
"I was in a cast that went from my toe to my hip, so my entire leg was locked up for three months," Diawara said. "The doctors told me at that point that I wouldn't grow anymore and would be 5-foot-7 for the rest of my life."
At Boys Latin Charter High School in Philadelphia, Diawara played baseball until the pandemic shut down his sophomore season. One of his older brothers, a teacher and track coach at the school, suggested they start working out. Diawara took a 45-pound dumbbell from the basement into the backyard and started doing curls and push-ups.
Two of his other brothers were playing college football when they came home during the shutdown. They lifted with him and helped him work on his footwork. By summer, Diawara was a little over 6 feet tall.
"I got two years of high school left. Why not just work my tail off?" he said. "If I'm going to do this, I'm going to do this full speed ahead. No games."
Through Focused Athletics, a Philadelphia nonprofit, he got help preparing for the SAT and trained alongside Division I players. Some afternoons he went straight from baseball practice to seven-on-seven football.
On his first day in full pads at Boys Latin, Diawara lined up at linebacker. The first play was a run up the middle. Diawara recalls the ball carrier weighing about 230 pounds.
"I'm either going to make this tackle or he's going to run through me," Diawara remembered thinking. "It wasn't a crazy hit stick or anything, but I made the tackle," he said. "I finished strong, and it was just like, 'Oh, this is right back where I left it. This is the same game I was playing when I was in middle school.'"
That was the confidence boost that Diawara needed.
By the summer before his senior year, Diawara had played five high school football games. He and one of his brothers got caught in traffic on the way to a recruiting camp at Monmouth University in New Jersey. They showed up about 20 minutes late and missed most of the drills, but there was still time for one-on-ones. Diawara remembers going 13-1 in drills against a group that included players who already had college offers.
"I got the video recordings still to this day of me just winning, winning, winning," he said.
Diawara earned all-state honors at defensive end that fall, but coaches kept telling him he was still raw. He spent his first year of college at Shippensburg practicing without playing in games. When he got his chance in 2023, he recorded six sacks.
Some of the schools that showed interest in high school were calling again. Diawara took out loans to go to Shippensburg and watched his brothers borrow to pay for college, too. Transferring gave him a chance to ease some of that cost for his family.
"I was just like, 'All right, I can help change my people's life, and I can make things a lot easier on myself so that this way I'm not just stacking up loans going to school,'" he said.
He chose Coastal.
When Diawara started rehab at Coastal, safety
Dre Pinckney
was recovering from a torn ACL. Pinckney had been in the program since 2021. He suffered a season-ending injury in the 2022 opener against Army, then came back the following fall and earned a spot on the Comeback College Football Player of the Year watch list.
"Me and Dre would go to rehab together every day," Diawara said. "My family was super supportive during that time, and they were trying to keep my spirits up and stuff. But if I had to pick one person that helped me get through that journey, it was Dre."
Some weeks, they did the same exercises every day.
"Sometimes things are out of your control," Diawara remembers Pinckney telling him. "Control what you can."
Diawara set out to learn the playbook before he was cleared to practice. That fall, he blocked a punt against William & Mary, but he was still getting only a handful of plays on defense.
"It was almost like another redshirt year for me," he said.
Diawara needed a second procedure on his right knee before spring practice in 2025. This time, he remembers missing only the first couple days of spring practices.
"By the time I got back out there, I looked like I belonged," he said. "I looked like I'm one of the guys. … I'm playing with guys that are real good players, but I'm like, 'I'm one of these good players, too.'
Diawara played in all 13 games on defense that season. Pinckney, who worked alongside him as they recovered from knee injuries the year before, was named a Coastal captain in 2025 and is still with the team as a graduate student. Diawara had also watched his older brother Mohamed become a captain at Marist University in New York.
"I watched my brother become a captain at his school and Dre become a captain here. I'm like, 'That's something I want to do,'" Diawara said. "I watched them go through it, and they faced so much adversity in their lives. I faced adversity, but they faced adversity, too. They never shied away from it and they always walked towards the storm."
When Coastal changed head coaches after the 2025 season, Diawara had one year left to play. He thought back to those workouts with his brothers and what he told himself when he decided to give football another shot. If he was going to do it, he wanted to see it through. He believed he could get better under
Ryan Beard
and his staff, so he stayed.
"I felt like I was going to take the next step up and be a captain," he said. "That was one of the goals I had written on my mirror, to be a captain."
In February, new defensive coordinator and defensive line coach
L.D. Scott
was getting ready to organize voluntary Saturday workouts for his linemen. Then the strength staff told him Diawara had already set up extra workouts with his teammates.
"Before I could even organize my guys into doing something on their own, he had already done it," Scott said. "And when I heard that from our strength and conditioning coaches, and this is within a couple weeks of getting to know the kid, I knew he was going to be a leader for our group."
Before the season, Diawara was named one of Coastal's four captains. Four games in, he already had more sacks than he recorded in his first two years at Coastal combined. He leads the team with five sacks and has 7.5 tackles for loss heading into Saturday's game against Arkansas State.
Before kickoff, he'll walk onto the field at Brooks Stadium for his final home game as a Chanticleer. Not only as a captain, but as a Coastal graduate. Off the field, he's been working on his MBA.
"You've got those two little guys on your shoulders," Diawara said. "One's in your ear saying, 'You can pull up. You don't have to run all the way through the line.' And the other one's saying, 'Nah, you've got to run all the way through the line and then some.' It hasn't been easy, but it was worth it."
